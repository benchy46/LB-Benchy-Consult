# #r5_dock-mech-design
**Slack channel ID:** C094T008CEB
**Purpose:** Mechanical design channel for the **R5 docking station** — tank geometry and capacity, the chemical-dosing module (peristaltic pump, check valves, dosing-ratio control), panel controls/HMI, rotomoulded body details, and sink/mesh-bowl design. Benjamin is a senior reviewer here rather than the day-to-day designer; the hands-on owners are Arunraj Janakiraman, Prabu B, Wei Hua, Sadheedh Jiffry and Lucas De Rijk.

**Added 2026-09-23.** New channel, missed by the original 50-channel seed despite ~13 months of his participation.

## Decisions & Reasoning (Benjamin)

- **[2026-09-17] Rejected an unsupported flat section, proposed the rib, and named backward compatibility as the price of the alternative — all in two sentences.** On a sink/mesh-bowl revision: "that long flat piece is unsupported, unless we can reenforce with a rib? Lets check if we can put a rib on the underside then maybe possible, while considering moldability. I understand theres a hole at the rear, we can change the cutout on the rotomold to make it not obvious, but that will have backward compatibility problems."
  - Three moves in order, each characteristic: (1) the structural objection stated as a fact with the fix attached in the same breath — *unsupported, unless we rib it*; (2) the fix immediately qualified by the constraint that could kill it — "while considering moldability," because a rib on a rotomoulded underside is not free; (3) the alternative path costed out loud before anyone pursues it — changing the rotomould cutout works cosmetically but breaks fielded units. He never says no; he prices each branch and hands back a decision.
  - Lucas replied with the team's direction (cut the sink bigger, to fix water sloshing out and insufficient mesh-bowl capacity) and picked up the constraint Benjamin had flagged, verbatim: "keeping in mind, backward compatiblilty may still bite us back," with Joei Wee checking with sales and RST whether it can be ignored. Note the shape of the resolution — backward compatibility is not settled inside engineering, it is escalated to the functions holding the field and customer picture. — [source](https://lionsbot.slack.com/archives/C094T008CEB/p1789656885648789)

### Earlier evidence (2025-08 → 2025-09, read as a sample when this file was created)

- **[2025-09-04] "Reverify first, then design" — stated as the rule, not the request.** After a check-valve flow test produced a result he distrusted ("Hmm really? Sounds not possible, or smth is wrong with the test"), he put the sequencing plainly: "Which is why, we need to reverify first before we design. So, confirm if you really need the check valve first. Then design accordingly." The component's *necessity* is the open question, and he will not let packaging design proceed around a part that may not be needed.
- **[2025-08-28] Specified how to test a component they do not have, using what is on the bench.** On the chemical-dosing check valve: "you must do a physical test first with the current check valves on hand and test the actual back pressure of the chemical dosing module. You can put a few check valves in series to simulate higher cracking pressure since we do not have a high pressure check valve." Rather than accept "we cannot test it without the right part," he constructs the missing spec out of parts in hand — series stacking to synthesise cracking pressure.
- **[2025-09-01] Revised his own risk read within the hour, unprompted.** First: "I think as long as the chemical tank is visible on the exterior, then it's acceptable. There's not much risk to clean without chemical anyways, I wouldn't think." Then, 56 minutes later: "On second thought, the risk would be the chemical pump runs dry for extended periods of time, which can reduce the life time of the chemical pump, not sure by how much also." He reopens his own clearance, names the real failure mode (dry-running the pump, not a cleaning-quality miss), and is explicit about the size of what he does not know.
- **[2025-09-02 → 09-05] Argued for discrete dosing settings on user-error grounds, then conceded to a simpler design once the cost of detents was priced by someone who had built them.** Opening position: "For Dosing ratio we won't want to have continous control as that can very easily let the user select wrong ratios. We were thinking about those step wise rotary selection knobs, to select a few preset ratios instead," reinforced with a concrete precedent — "I'm slightly afraid that you will come to that situation with the red safety relay on SC25, that you select it but it's not selected kinda thing." When Prabu B reported real experience with detent mechanisms ("it needs some iterations on the springs and detent surfaces... we had lots of field issues in that assembly because of manufacturing tolerances"), he folded: "Assuming they won't touch it frequently, and it's somewhat hidden, I guess it can be acceptable. Simpler also better. Some feedback from the LED's that the dosing ratio has changed would be possible too."
  - The concession is textbook for him: a colleague's *field-failure* experience beats his own theoretical user-error argument, he concedes in one line, endorses the simpler option explicitly ("Simpler also better"), and preserves the underlying requirement — the user still needs feedback that the setting changed — by relocating it to an LED instead of to geometry. Same structure as every other documented concession of his: the confidence argument goes, the requirement stays.
  - Before conceding he had offered a build route for the detents anyway: "With some engineering you can use a normal Pot, and then use geometry to make the Pot have steps as per mechanical design... You also will want to use radial geometrial constraints" / "Basically snap fit, geometry can be chunky to last longer." Note the instinct — get the discrete behaviour from *geometry around a standard cheap part* rather than from a more expensive specified component.
- **[2025-09-04] Answered a pin-count/cost debate by asking what resolution is actually required.** "It depends on the resolution that you need eventually. If you use a binary system then you can use fewer wires. If you use ADC, You may have a more complicated and expensive switch. 4 pins or 5 pins, the additional connector cost is actually how much?" — reframes a component argument as a requirements question, and challenges the cost objection by asking for its magnitude rather than accepting it.
- **[2025-08-27] Gave a design direction as an objective plus a constraint, not a shape.** On the docking-station tanks: "we will want to Maximise as much volume towards the front of the station, so the shape will be smth like offset surface from the docking station front face. And possibly introduce a pokeyoke installation of the tanks at the same time." The poka-yoke suggestion arrives, as it usually does, attached to work already being done — the tanks are being reshaped anyway, so making misinstallation impossible is nearly free.

## Recurring Themes / Principles Observed
- **Reviewer, not owner, in this channel** — he answers with rulings and reframes rather than CAD, and repeatedly hands the decision back once he has priced the branches.
- **Necessity before design**: confirm the component is needed before packaging around it; confirm the requirement's resolution before choosing the part that meets it.
- **Test with what is on hand** — synthesise a missing spec (check valves in series) rather than wait for the right part to arrive.
- **Reopens his own clearances** when a second failure mode occurs to him, and states the bound of his own ignorance ("not sure by how much also").
- **Concedes to lived field-failure experience faster than to any other argument**, while relocating the requirement he was protecting rather than dropping it.
- **Names backward compatibility as a first-class cost** of a moulded-part change, and escalates that call to sales/service rather than deciding it in engineering.
